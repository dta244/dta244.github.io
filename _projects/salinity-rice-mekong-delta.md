## The decision

Sea-level rise is pushing saltwater further into the Mekong Delta, Vietnam's main rice-growing region, and
many coastal fields are becoming less suited to rice. Farmers can adapt rice production in place, with
salt-tolerant varieties and salinity barriers, or move their land into other uses. Policy has tended to back
one path or the other: large infrastructure that locks farmers into intensive rice, or support for finding
alternative land uses. Choosing between them needs evidence on how much salinity actually costs rice farmers
and what makes them stop growing rice.

## Approach

The project surveyed rice-growing households in salinity-prone areas of the delta across several cropping
years, building field-level panel data. The first paper uses a two-year panel of 788 households. Fixed-effects
regressions estimate how salt-tolerant varieties affect yields, and probit and IV-probit models explain which
fields leave rice production. The second paper uses three years of data from 758 households to estimate how
salinity exposure affects field-level rice profits and how expected profits shape decisions to exit rice.

## What this supports

* Salt-tolerant varieties raise yields on fields not protected by salinity barriers, but their lower market
  prices limit the overall economic gain.
* Farmers stopped growing rice on 15% of surveyed fields. Falling profitability drives these exits, while
  salinity barriers, large rice holdings, and community commitment to rice farming are associated with
  staying in rice.
* High salinity cuts average field profits by more than 75%. Exits rise once expected profits fall below
  about 20 million VND per hectare, and farmers form those expectations from both the latest season and
  past extreme salinity events.

The first paper concludes that support for adaptation should blend the two polarized policy options,
investment in rice infrastructure and support for farmers moving to other land uses, rather than choose
between them.
